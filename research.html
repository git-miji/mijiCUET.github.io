Research

Confining light with metal

Plasmonics concentrates optical energy into volumes far below the diffraction limit. My work models how nanoscale geometry controls that confinement — and what it costs in loss.

Motivation

Why plasmonics

Conventional optics cannot focus light much below half a wavelength. Metals change that. When light drives the free electrons at a metal–dielectric interface, it excites a surface plasmon — a coupled charge-and-field oscillation that confines optical energy into volumes far smaller than the diffraction limit.

That confinement is the whole point. It concentrates field intensity into nanometre gaps, which is what makes single-molecule Raman sensing, sub-wavelength waveguiding, and ultra-thin optical elements possible. The cost is loss: metals absorb. Nearly every interesting design problem in this field is a negotiation between confinement and loss, and that trade-off sits at the centre of my doctoral research.

Because analytic solutions exist only for the simplest geometries, progress depends on trustworthy numerics. My emphasis is on simulations that are convergence-tested and cross-validated between independent solvers — not a single pretty field plot.

Research areas

Five threads I am pursuing

01

Localised surface plasmon resonance in coupled nanostructures

A single metal nanoparticle has a resonance set by its size, shape and surroundings. Bring a second particle close and the modes hybridise — the resonance red-shifts and the field in the gap intensifies sharply as the separation closes. I model how geometry controls that enhancement, and where the classical description begins to break down at sub-nanometre gaps.

LSPRMode hybridisation Gap dependenceHot-spots
02

Metasurfaces and resonant nanoantenna arrays

Arrays of sub-wavelength resonators can impose an arbitrary phase profile on a wavefront within a single layer — replacing bulk optics with something essentially flat. I am interested in the sharp, asymmetric Fano lineshapes that arise when a broad bright mode interferes with a narrow dark one, because the resulting high-Q features are exactly what sensing and narrowband filtering need.

MetasurfacesFano resonance High-QWavefront shaping
03

Rigorous computational electromagnetics

A plasmonic simulation can look plausible and still be wrong. Metals demand fine meshing, dispersive material models must actually fit the measured permittivity over the band of interest, and absorbing boundaries have to be verified rather than assumed. My practice is to run resolution convergence studies, check PML reflections, and reproduce key results in a second, independent solver before trusting a number.

FDTDConvergence testing Dispersive fittingPML validation
04

Machine learning for photonic design

Full-wave sweeps over a multi-parameter design space are expensive. Trained on a well-chosen set of simulations, a surrogate model can predict spectra in milliseconds and turn optimisation — and eventually inverse design — into something tractable. This is where my machine-learning work connects directly to photonics, rather than sitting beside it.

Surrogate modelsInverse design Neural networksKolmogorov–Arnold networks
05

Plasmonic sensing and waveguiding

Two applications drive much of the above. In sensing, the detection limit follows the field enhancement in the gap and the sharpness of the resonance. In waveguiding, hybrid plasmonic–dielectric geometries try to keep useful mode confinement while pushing propagation length far enough to be practical on a chip.

SERSRefractive-index sensing Hybrid waveguidesIntegrated photonics

Method

How I run a study

A repeatable workflow matters more than any individual result. This is the sequence I follow for every simulation.

STEP 01

Define & validate

Set geometry, sources and monitors. Fit the dispersive material model and check it against published permittivity data over the target band.

STEP 02

Converge

Sweep mesh resolution, domain size and PML thickness until the quantity of interest stops moving. Record the convergence curve.

STEP 03

Sweep & analyse

Run the parameter space — on GPU cloud where the volume is large — then extract spectra, Q factors and enhancement maps.

STEP 04

Cross-check & document

Reproduce headline results in the second solver, then publish scripts and figures so the study can be repeated exactly.
